Win rate vs expectancy: how to tell what a trading result really means

“It wins most of the time” sounds reassuring. It leaves out how much the winners make, how much the losers cost and what happens between them. Those are the questions to ask before putting a signal service or indicator into your routine.

By Taylor Bright · Founder, Perfect EntryPublished 5 November 20265 min read
The short answer

Win rate measures how often trades win. Expectancy measures the average outcome per trade. With no flat trades, expectancy equals win probability × average win minus loss probability × average loss. Use realised outcomes and include costs; a high win rate alone does not establish profitability.

Start with a consistent unit: R

One R is the amount of risk planned for a trade at entry. If your planned initial risk is £50, a £100 gain is +2R and a £50 loss is −1R. Actual losses can exceed −1R when execution slips or a gap passes the stop.

Recording each trade in R makes differently sized trades easier to compare. Divide each realised net outcome by that trade’s own initial planned risk. Do not divide all trades by the most recent risk amount or change the denominator after seeing the result.

A target of 3R is not a realised average win of 3R. Partial exits, early closures and failed targets change the result. Evaluate the outcomes the method actually recorded.

Calculate expectancy from wins and losses

For a simple set with no flat trades, let p be the proportion of winners, W the average winning outcome in R, and L the positive magnitude of the average loss in R.

Expectancy = (p × W) − ((1 − p) × L)

If there are breakeven or other outcomes, the most direct calculation is the sum of every net R outcome divided by the total number of trades. Include zero outcomes in that count. Define what counts as a trade so that partial exits do not artificially inflate the win rate.

CME Group’s discussion of mathematical expectation explains the central distinction: trading outcomes do not all carry the same payoff. Our numbers below are invented teaching examples, not Perfect Entry performance.

Method A produces 28R from winners and loses 30R. Method B produces 80R and loses 60R. The method with the lower win rate has the better arithmetic in this example. That does not make lower win rates inherently better: change the payouts and the comparison changes.

There is also no account growth percentage implied by this table. Account results depend on the risk sizing, trade order, overlapping exposure and compounding rules.

Add costs and calculate the breakeven threshold

If the averages above exclude costs, an additional average cost of 0.08R per trade would reduce Method B’s expectancy from +0.20R to +0.12R. Across the same 100 trades that is 8R of friction. If the recorded returns already include those costs, do not subtract them twice.

For fixed winning and losing payoffs, with no flats or additional costs, the breakeven win rate is L ÷ (W + L). With 2R wins and 1R losses, that is one-third, approximately 33.3%. With 0.4R wins and 1R losses, it is approximately 71.4%.

These are thresholds under the stated payoff assumptions. They do not predict the probability of the next trade. A method targeting 2R but realising smaller winners needs a calculation based on those smaller outcomes.

An average still leaves important questions unanswered

Two records can have the same expectancy and very different paths. Check maximum drawdown, the length of adverse periods, the contribution of unusually large winners and the number of independent observations. Ten positions all expressing the same currency view are not ten unrelated pieces of evidence.

Check how trades are selected and what is missing. Are open positions excluded? Were rules changed during the sample? Is the record backtested, forward tracked or based on actual broker executions? Do timestamps and costs make the result reproducible?

A positive historical average is a description of that sample. It does not establish that the same average will persist. Use the backtesting checklist to examine how the sample was produced.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 70% win rate lose money?

Yes. In the hypothetical example, 70 wins averaging +0.4R and 30 losses averaging −1R produce −2R before separately modelled costs.

Is a positive expectancy guaranteed to continue?

No. A historical average may change with market conditions, costs, execution or statistical variation.

Should breakeven trades count?

Include them when calculating the average net outcome per trade, and disclose how they are treated in the win-rate calculation.
